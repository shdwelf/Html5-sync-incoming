EASTER EGGS hidden messages buried in software and film.

Chapter 03 — Easter Eggs

An easter egg is a deliberate, hidden feature or message tucked inside a product — usually playful, usually undocumented, and meant to be discovered. They exist across software and (as behind-the-scenes trivia) in films. Two wonderful public sources of record are the Easter Egg Archive (eeggs.com) and fan-run collections of movie-trivia "eggs."

Eggs in software: MS-DOS examples

The MS-DOS easter-egg list on the Easter Egg Archive collects several small, harmless jokes tucked into the operating system of the late 1980s / early 1990s:

  • The "no help" gag. In some versions of Microsoft's system diagnostics utility (MSD.EXE), pressing F1 on the About screen produced a joke instead of the help you'd expect — a wink at how empty that help actually was.
  • The timestamp joke. Every file shipped in MS-DOS 6.22 was stamped to match the version number — all showing the same 6:22 time — a quiet signature across the whole release.
  • Message games. Several system tools hid cheeky responses when invoked in unexpected ways, part of the era's friendly tone.

I'm describing these from the archive in original words; visit eeggs.com for the exact keystrokes and screenshots. Old eggs like these make great, safe things to try in an emulator (e.g. MS-DOS in DOSBox).

Eggs in film: behind-the-scenes trivia

Movies don't usually have "easter eggs" in the code sense, but fan archivists use the word for hidden jokes and behind-the-scenes details planted in a production. A good example is the unofficial fan site for the National Treasure films (nationaltreasure.us/easter-eggs), which catalogs details fans love to rewatch for: prop choices that wink at history, running jokes between characters, and hidden references in the clue-hunting set pieces. These are the "eggs" of the movie world — small, human touches that reward a close second viewing.

Note on rights. This site describes such eggs in its own words and does not reproduce any studio's or archive's original text or imagery. If you want the exact entries, follow the linked fan archives.

Why eggs matter

Easter eggs humanise a product. They are the signature of the people who built it — proof that behind the interface there was someone having fun. Preserving them (and the stories of finding them) is a small but real part of computing and film history.
