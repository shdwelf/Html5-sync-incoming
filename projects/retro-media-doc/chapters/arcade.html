Chapter 01 — The Coin-Op Arcade

Before home consoles there was the coin-operated arcade cabinet — a wood-and-glass box that turned a quarter into three minutes of neon. Today much of its history is preserved the way sports records are: by fans, collectors, and the community behind the Museum of the Game (better known by its database's old initials, KLOV — the Killer List of Videogames).

Why the arcade mattered

The cabinet was a shared, social screen. High scores were public and legible — your initials on a board everyone could see. That competition plus spectacle trained a generation of players and, later, of programmers and designers. Arcade games pushed hardware to its limits, and the techniques used to squeeze more onto the screen (scrolling, sprites, colour cycling, raster tricks) flowed directly into home systems and eventually into the demo scene covered in the next chapter.

The Museum of the Game

The arcade-museum.com site is a large, collector-built database of coin-operated machines: video games, pinball, and more. Its value is archival — it tracks thousands of titles, manufacturers, and even historical trade magazines (its "eLibrary" hosts pages of scanned coin-op periodicals). If you want to chase a specific cabinet's lineage, that's where the community record lives.

Try it: use the Museum of the Game's search to look up a game from your childhood, then compare its entry to its "attract mode" footage on the Internet Archive's arcade emulation collections.
